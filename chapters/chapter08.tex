\section{The Development of Theory of Mind}

Perspective taking is not the same as an act of goodwill; it represents a profound cognitive milestone.
At its core lies Theory of Mind (ToM)---the ability to recognize that others possess beliefs, desires, and intentions distinct from one's own.
While foundational research by \citet{wimmer1983} and early meta-analyses by \citet{wellman2001} established false-belief understanding as a critical leap occurring around age four, developmental psychology views ToM as a continuous, dynamic process.
Recent advancements, such as the Early Sociocognitive Battery for 18-month-olds, highlight how foundational interactive skills set the stage for later cognitive empathy \citep{roy2024}.
These early traits show remarkable longitudinal stability, proving that an infant's early social understanding strongly predicts their later preschool competence \citep{sodian2024}.
Crucially, ToM has deep relational origins; developmental trajectories are heavily influenced by the quality of a parent-child mutually responsive orientation and the caregiver's verbal behavior mirroring the child's internal states \citep{kochanska2025, espinosa2025}.

Understanding this expanded developmental timeline is essential for empathetic parenting.
When a toddler seems dismissive of a parent's frustration, it is not inherently an act of defiance, but often a reflection of their developing neurocognitive architecture navigating a cross-culturally shaped cognitive sequence \citep{yu2024}.
Furthermore, ToM maturation continues well past early childhood; in middle childhood, children are still refining their ability to navigate complex social norms, peer dynamics, and hidden emotional states \citep{smogorzewska2024, osterhaus2024, lecce2024}.
Recognizing this extended growth phase allows parents to replace judgement with observation, a core tenet of Nonviolent Communication (NVC).
Instead of labeling a child as ``selfish,'' parents can observe their child's developing neurocognitive architecture.
As children grow, they increasingly distinguish between ``cognitive'' ToM (understanding mental states) and ``affective'' ToM (empathizing with feelings), an ability that continues to evolve across the human lifespan \citep{cornaggia2024}.

\begin{keyinsight}
Theory of Mind is a lifelong cognitive milestone with deep relational origins shaped by parent-child responsiveness. 
Recognizing that both cognitive and affective ToM continuously mature through middle childhood empowers parents to replace judgements of ``selfishness'' with empathetic observations.
\end{keyinsight}

\section{Why Child Struggles with Perspective Taking?}

For children, the inability to take another's perspective is not born of selfishness, stubbornness, or a lack of love; it is fundamentally a matter of cognitive constraints and neurobiological immaturity. 
Recent research across developmental psychology and cognitive neuroscience provides a clear picture of why shifting perspectives is intensely demanding for the developing brain.

The primary hurdle lies in the delayed maturation of the brain's executive function networks, specifically the prefrontal cortex. 
To take another person's perspective, a child must first actively suppress their own deeply felt reality---a process known as inhibitory control. 
Studies show that overriding this ``egocentric bias'' imposes a severe cognitive cost on preschoolers, requiring levels of inhibitory control that they have only just begun to develop \citep{blankenship2024, carlson2024b}. 
Similarly, holding both their own perspective and someone else’s simultaneously demands immense working memory capacity. 
When working memory limits are exceeded, children default back to their own immediate experience \citep{richardson2025}. 
Information processing bottlenecks constrain how much social data a child can synthesize at once, making complex social interactions highly prone to misunderstandings \citep{gweon2024}.

Cognitive flexibility, or the ability to smoothly shift attention between the ``own-knowledge'' perspective and a different viewpoint, relies on regions of the brain that are still under construction for children. 
Children frequently display a rigid adherence to their own knowledge simply because shifting mental gears requires fully matured neural networks \citep{zaitchik2025, moriguchi2025}. 
Recent fMRI studies also highlight that mentalizing regions, such as the Temporoparietal Junction (TPJ) and the Default Mode Network, undergo significant structural refinement throughout early to middle childhood; an underdeveloped TPJ makes it neurologically impossible for a child to consistently generate an accurate model of another's mind \citep{saxe2025}.

Emotional regulation also plays a pivotal role in perspective taking. 
When a child is stressed, tired, or emotionally dysregulated, amygdala activation can actively inhibit the prefrontal networks responsible for cognitive empathy \citep{thompson2026b}. 
Environmental stressors and elevated daytime cortisol have been explicitly linked to delays in both executive function and Theory of Mind performance \citep{devine2024}. 
Therefore, when a child is overwhelmed by a tantrum, asking them to ``think about how the other person feels'' is fighting against their biology.

Understanding these structural constraints helps parents replace evaluations like ``you are being unreasonable'' with observations like ``my child's prefrontal cortex is currently overloaded.'' 
When we recognize that perspective taking is a monumentally complex neuro-cognitive task, we can approach our children’s egocentric moments with patience, offering the regulatory support they need until their brains can bridge the gap on their own.

\begin{keyinsight}
A child's struggle with perspective taking is rooted in neurobiological immaturity, not selfishness. 
Neuroscience development highlights that immense demands on inhibitory control, working memory, and not-yet-matured brain regions (like the TPJ and prefrontal cortex) could make holding another's viewpoint cognitively exhausting for children.
\end{keyinsight}

\section{Why Parents Could Also Struggle with Perspective Taking?}

While we often attribute perspective-taking struggles to the developing brains of our children, parents are equally susceptible to losing this capacity. 
Even with a fully mature prefrontal cortex, a parent's ability to engage in cognitive empathy is highly vulnerable to stress, emotional triggers, and cognitive overload.

The adult brain relies on the prefrontal cortex to practice perspective taking---a resource-intensive process. 
When parents are overwhelmed by the relentless demands of daily life, sleep deprivation, or task saturation, they experience cognitive depletion. 
Under these conditions of chronic stress, the brain's ``social engagement system'' is compromised \citep{porges2011}. 
In an effort to conserve mental energy, the brain shifts from higher-order reflective thinking into evolutionary survival modes. 
The amygdala activates a fight-or-flight response, effectively hijacking the prefrontal cortex and severely limiting the parent's capacity to see beyond their own immediate needs or frustrations.

Furthermore, perspective taking in parenting is heavily entangled with a parent's own unhealed childhood wounds. 
When a child exhibits a behavior---such as a loud tantrum or fierce defiance---it can unconsciously trigger implicit memories of similar events from the parent's past \citep{siegel2003}. 
If a parent was raised in an environment where anger was met with harsh punishment or emotional dismissal, their child's anger will likely trigger an automatic, subconscious defense mechanism. 
In these highly activated moments, parents project their own unresolved fears onto the child, making perspective taking virtually impossible. 
They are no longer responding to the child in front of them, but rather reacting to the echoes of their own past \citep{vanderkolk2014}.

Additionally, parents often suffer from the ``illusion of asymmetric insight''---a cognitive bias where caregivers believe they understand their child's internal world better than the child does. 
This assumption leads parents to invalidate the child's actual experience, assuming that if the child’s perspective does not match their own adult logic, it must be inherently flawed or manipulative. 
Parents could replace their evaluations with neutral observation and curiosity, acknowledging that we can never fully inhabit another person's subjective reality \citep{rosenberg2003}. 

This blind spot in parenting is frequently compounded by the Dunning-Kruger effect, a cognitive bias in which individuals with limited knowledge in a specific domain drastically overestimate their own competence \citep{kruger1999}. 
In the context of parenting, caregivers who are largely unaware of the neurobiological complexities of child development are often the most confident that their disciplinary approaches and interpretations of a child's behavior are objectively correct. 
This inflated self-assessment creates a rigid psychological barrier. 
Because the parent feels absolute certainty in their evaluation (e.g., ``he is just trying to make me angry''), they feel no need to engage their prefrontal cortex to genuinely seek out or consider the child's actual underlying needs. 
Perspective taking requires intellectual humility---an acknowledgment that there is always more to a child's internal world than what is visible on the surface.

When parents lose the capacity to hold multiple perspectives, relationships easily devolve into binary power struggles. 
Recognizing our own neurobiological limits is an act of profound self-empathy. 
By understanding that our own perspective-taking faculties can be temporarily disabled by stress or trauma, we can prioritize self-regulation and pause before responding, ensuring we re-engage our prefrontal cortex before addressing our child's needs.

\begin{keyinsight}
Parents could also struggle with perspective taking due to cognitive depletion, amygdala activation under stress, and the triggering of their own unresolved childhood wounds. 
Recognizing these limitations---along with cognitive biases like the illusion of asymmetric insight and the Dunning-Kruger effect---shifts the focus from parental guilt and rigid certainty to self-empathy, intellectual humility, and proactive self-regulation.
\end{keyinsight}

\section{Narcissistic Personality Disorder}

While stress or unhealed wounds can temporarily compromise any parent's ability to engage in perspective taking, chronically unaddressed family dynamics and untreated mental trauma could lead to a long-term mental disorder. 
Perspective taking abilities are more profoundly and persistently impaired in individuals with Narcissistic Personality Disorder (NPD).
From a psychological and neurobiological standpoint, NPD is characterized by severe deficits in empathy architectures, specifically oscillating between an extreme, rigid focus on the self and an acute defensive fragility.
When compounded with the Dunning-Kruger effect mentioned earlier, parents with NPD are often unaware of their limitations, making it extremely difficult for them to recognize their child's perspective and validate feelings of a child.

Studies highlight what is known as the ``empathy paradox'' in NPD: parents with pathological narcissism often possess an intact ``cognitive'' Theory of Mind---meaning they can intellectually recognize what their child is thinking or feeling---but they demonstrate a deficient ``affective resonance,'' struggling to genuinely share or care about that emotional state \citep{baskin2025, ronningstam2024}.
Because the emotional regulation systems in individuals with NPD are highly reactive and structurally dysregulated, a child's differing needs are rarely perceived as separate, valid experiences.
Instead, these needs are frequently interpreted as direct threats to the parent's fragile ego or as deliberate acts of defiance \citep{day2024}.

In the context of the family dynamic, a parent with NPD traits tends to view the child not as an autonomous individual, but as an extension of themselves.
When a child asserts independence or expresses an emotion that conflicts with the narcissistic parent's preferred narrative, the parent's underdeveloped empathy networks fail to bridge the gap.
They resort to projecting their own unresolved shame, anger, or anxiety onto the child, entirely obliterating the child's perspective in the process.
This dynamic forces the child to become hyper-vigilant, constantly adapting their own reality to manage the parent's emotional volatility rather than developing a secure sense of self.

For the co-parent or the adult child of a parent with NPD, understanding these neurocognitive limitations is essential for protecting one's own emotional boundaries.
Instead of evaluating the narcissistic parent as inherently ``evil'' or ``toxic''---labels that keep us trapped in anger and powerlessness---we can make neutral observations about their neurobiological capability.
For example, a co-parent might observe: ``I see that when our child cries, it triggers intense frustration in you, and you demand immediate compliance. 
It helps me to understand that your empathy networks are currently unable to hold space for their sadness without feeling personally attacked.''
This shift from judgment to observation does not excuse harmful behavior or remove accountability.
Rather, it allows us to set firm, self-protective boundaries grounded in the reality of their cognitive limitations, freeing us from the exhausting expectation that they will one day suddenly develop the capacity for profound empathy.

Seeking professional help is a crucial, courageous step for parents who recognize their struggle with empathy and perspective-taking. 
Because these empathy deficits often stem from deeply entrenched neurobiological patterns and unhealed childhood trauma, they are rarely resolved through sheer willpower or generic parenting advice. 
Evidence-based therapeutic modalities, particularly Mentalization-Based Treatment (MBT) and Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT), have shown significant efficacy in helping individuals reconstruct their capacity for affective resonance and perspective taking \citep{bateman2024, linehan2014}. 
MBT explicitly focuses on strengthening a parent's ability to ``mentalize''---to consciously interpret their own and their child's behavior as being driven by underlying mental states, feelings, and needs. 
By working with a trained professional, parents can safely explore the root causes of their emotional triggers in a non-judgmental space. 
Therapy provides the scaffolding needed to practice emotional regulation, allowing parents to slowly dismantle their ego-defenses and rebuild the neural pathways required for genuine, empathetic connection with their children.

\begin{keyinsight}
Parents with chronically unaddressed family dynamics or untreated trauma may develop NPD, leading them to perceive a child's differing needs as direct assaults on their ego or authority.
Understanding that these empathy deficits are rooted in structural and neurobiological dysregulation empowers family members to replace moral judgements (e.g., ``toxic'') with objective observations, facilitating firm boundaries without the burden of expecting sustained empathetic connection.
\end{keyinsight}

\section{Conversational Techniques for Encouraging Empathy}

Cultivating a child's capacity for perspective taking goes beyond simply instructing them to ``think about how others feel.'' 
It requires scaffolding their developing neurobiology through consistent, empathetic communication. 
Studies in neuro-linguistics and developmental psychology demonstrate that the physical architecture of a child's ``social brain'' is heavily shaped by the quality of daily conversations, specifically through the mechanics of back-and-forth dialogue and emotional validation.

The foundation of empathetic communication is the ``conversational turn''---the alternating rhythm of speaking and listening between parent and child.
While early research emphasized the sheer volume of words a child hears, contemporary longitudinal studies confirm that conversational turns are the true catalyst for socioemotional cognitive growth \citep{romeo2024}.
These ``serve and return'' interactions stimulate cortical thickness in brain regions associated with both language processing and Theory of Mind.
When a parent mindfully responds to a child's utterance with genuine curiosity, rather than a dismissive or corrective statement, they are actively firing the neural pathways the child needs to process complex, multi-person interactions.
Through frequent back-and-forth dialogue, children internalize the fundamental social rules of reciprocity, patience, and mutual respect, which form the bedrock of perspective taking.

Beyond the structural mechanics of conversation, the emotional content of the dialogue is equally critical. 
Emotion coaching, a framework initially conceptualized by \citet{gottman1997}, has been empirically shown to enhance affective resonance in parent-child dyads \citep{schimmel2025}.
The core technique involves a parent unconditionally validating a child's emotional state before attempting to correct their behavior or offer practical solutions.
For instance, instead of saying, ``There is no reason to cry over a broken toy,'' an empathetic parent might say, ``I can see you are feeling very sad and frustrated that your toy broke.''
By mirroring the child's internal state without judgement or immediate problem-solving, the parent provides external co-regulation.
This validation down-regulates the child's amygdala activation, effectively freeing up cognitive resources in their prefrontal cortex so they can later engage in logical reasoning and broader perspective taking.

Furthermore, parents can intentionally construct their dialogue using the principles of Nonviolent Communication (NVC) to model perspective taking in real-time \citep{rosenberg2003}.
NVC encourages caregivers to strip their language of evaluations, labels, and blame, focusing exclusively on objective observations, feelings, and underlying needs.
When parents shift from interrogative demands (e.g., ``Why did you take your sister's book?'') to open-ended, need-based inquiries (e.g., ``Are you feeling upset because you needed a turn with that book?''), they guide the child's attention toward internal states rather than external conflicts.
\citet{nelson2025} show that maternal active listening---characterized by reflecting the child's perceived needs and delaying parental judgement---significantly accelerates preschoolers' Theory of Mind development.
By utilizing NVC, parents not only protect their child's ego from defensiveness but also provide a real-time linguistic template for how to simultaneously hold one's own perspective and respect another's.

\begin{keyinsight}
Perspective taking is actively built through the quality of daily parent-child dialogue.
Techniques such as engaging in frequent conversational turns, utilizing emotion coaching to validate feelings, and applying Nonviolent Communication to identify underlying needs directly stimulate the neurobiological networks required for empathy and secure attachment.
\end{keyinsight}

\textbf{Multiple Choice Questions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item When a three-year-old assumes you know what they are thinking about, this is primarily an example of:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Defiance against adult authority.
    \item A lack of emotional attachment.
    \item Normal egocentric thinking before Theory of Mind is fully developed.
    \item Manipulative behavior.
  \end{enumerate}
  \vspace{\fill}
  \item Which of the following is crucial for cognitive empathy?
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Completely merging with the other person's emotions.
    \item The capacity to distinguish between oneself and others.
    \item Ignoring one's own feelings to focus entirely on the child.
    \item Suppressing the child's negative emotions.
  \end{enumerate}
  \vspace{\fill}
  \item Replacing the label ``selfish'' with an observation about a child's developmental stage helps parents:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Excuse the child from any boundaries.
    \item Avoid addressing the behavior altogether.
    \item Respond with empathy and realistic expectations.
    \item Punish the child more effectively.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
\vspace{\fill}
\newpage

\textbf{Explanations for Questions 1--3}

\textbf{1. (c)} Normal egocentric thinking before Theory of Mind is fully developed. At this age, children typically have not yet realized that others have different knowledge and perspectives, a developmental milestone that usually solidifies around age four.

\textbf{2. (b)} The capacity to distinguish between oneself and others. Cognitive empathy requires understanding another's state without experiencing emotional contagion, allowing the parent to remain regulated while supporting the child.

\textbf{3. (c)} Respond with empathy and realistic expectations. By removing judgement and understanding the cognitive reasons behind a behavior, parents can connect with the child's underlying needs and address the situation without blame.
\vspace{\fill}

\textbf{Explanations for Questions 4--6}

\textbf{4. (c)} Recognizing that both the parent's and the child's feelings and needs can coexist simultaneously. This view allows for multiple truths and prevents interactions from becoming zero-sum power struggles.

\textbf{5. (b)} Emotional contagion. This occurs when one person automatically mirrors another's emotional state, losing the self-other distinction that is necessary for true cognitive empathy and effective co-regulation.

\textbf{6. (c)} ``I see you are sad to leave the park, and I am feeling rushed because we need to get home.'' This statement acknowledges both the child's feelings and the parent's feelings without blaming or evaluating the child.
