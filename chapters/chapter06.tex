\chapter{Nonviolent Communication with Children}

\begin{quote}
\itshape ``Every criticism, judgment, diagnosis, and expression of anger 
is the tragic expression of an unmet need.''\\
\upshape --- Marshall B.\ Rosenberg
\end{quote}

\section{The Language of Connection}

The words we use with children matter enormously---not only because of 
their immediate effect but because language shapes how children come to 
understand themselves, others, and relationships.

\citet{rosenberg2003} model of \textbf{Nonviolent Communication (NVC)} 
provides a powerful framework for speaking to children (and everyone else) 
in a way that fosters understanding, cooperation, and genuine connection. 
The model was developed from Rosenberg's \citep{rosenberg2003} work as a clinical psychologist 
and mediator, and has been applied in schools, families, and conflict zones 
worldwide.

\section{The Four Components of NVC}

NVC organizes any communication into four steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Observation}: What are you seeing or hearing---described 
        without evaluation or judgment?
  \item \textbf{Feeling}: What emotion arises in you in response to that 
        observation?
  \item \textbf{Need}: What underlying need of yours is connected to that 
        feeling?
  \item \textbf{Request}: What specific action would you like to happen?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Step 1: Observation Without Evaluation}

Evaluation masquerading as observation is one of the most common forms of 
communication violence. Compare:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{5.5cm}p{5.5cm}}
\hline
\textbf{Evaluation (harmful)} & \textbf{Observation (neutral)} \\
\hline
``You're so messy.'' & 
``I notice your clothes are on the floor.'' \\[4pt]
``You never listen to me.'' & 
``When I asked you to set the table, you continued 
watching TV.'' \\[4pt]
``You're being a brat.'' & 
``You hit your sister and then called her a name.'' \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{Step 2: Feeling}

NVC distinguishes between \textit{feelings} (genuine inner states) and 
\textit{pseudo-feelings} (evaluations of what others are doing, disguised 
as feelings).

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{5.5cm}p{5.5cm}}
\hline
\textbf{Pseudo-feeling (avoid)} & \textbf{Feeling (prefer)} \\
\hline
``I feel like you don't care.'' & ``I feel discouraged.'' \\
``I feel ignored.'' & ``I feel lonely.'' \\
``I feel manipulated.'' & ``I feel frustrated and worried.'' \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{Step 3: Need}

Behind every feeling is an unmet (or met) need. NVC identifies a set of 
universal human needs including:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Safety, security, and stability
  \item Belonging, love, and connection
  \item Autonomy and choice
  \item Contribution and meaning
  \item Rest, play, and creativity
  \item Respect and understanding
\end{itemize}

When you share a need, you give your child something to understand about 
you---not a character indictment, but a human truth.

\subsection{Step 4: Request}

A request is specific, positive (stating what you \textit{want}, not what 
you don't want), and presented as a genuine ask rather than a demand.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Demand: ``You will put your dishes in the sink right now.''
  \item Request: ``Would you be willing to put your dishes in the sink 
        before going to play?''
\end{itemize}

The difference is not just in tone but in intention. A demand signals that 
you expect obedience; a request signals that you expect cooperation from 
someone whose autonomy you respect.

\section{Putting It All Together}

\begin{quote}
``When I see your backpack, coat, and shoes in the middle of the hallway 
[observation], I feel stressed and a bit frantic [feeling] because I need 
the shared space to feel manageable [need]. Would you be willing to put 
your things in your room when you come home? [request]''
\end{quote}

This is not a script to follow rigidly---it is an orientation. In practice, 
NVC might be much briefer: ``I'm feeling overwhelmed by the mess. Can you 
help me out here?'' The spirit of the approach is what matters.

\section{Receiving NVC from Your Child}

NVC is not only about how you speak---it is equally about how you hear. 
When your child says ``You never let me do anything!'' the NVC-informed 
parent looks for the feeling and need beneath the complaint:

\begin{quote}
``It sounds like you're feeling really frustrated right now. Is it that 
you're wanting more say in what happens?''
\end{quote}

This is empathetic receiving: hearing the child's message at its deepest 
level rather than reacting to its surface.

\begin{keyinsight}
Every accusation is a needs statement in disguise. When your child says 
``You're so mean,'' they are saying ``I need something different from you 
right now.'' When you hear the need, you can respond to it.
\end{keyinsight}

\section{NVC and Conflict Between Siblings}

Sibling conflict is one of the most fertile laboratories for practicing NVC 
in family life. When two children are in conflict, an empathetic parent can 
serve as a translator:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Reflect each child's feeling: ``You're angry because\ldots'' / 
        ``You're hurt because\ldots''
  \item Identify each child's need: ``It sounds like you both need to feel 
        respected.''
  \item Invite joint problem-solving: ``What could work for both of you?''
\end{enumerate}

This approach takes longer than declaring a winner, but it teaches children 
that conflicts have solutions, and that their needs matter---as do the needs 
of others.

\begin{practiceexercise}[title=The NVC Letter]
Think of a recurring conflict or frustration with your child. Before your 
next conversation about it, write a brief NVC statement:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Observation}: What specifically do you observe?
  \item \textbf{Feeling}: What do you feel?
  \item \textbf{Need}: What need is connected to that feeling?
  \item \textbf{Request}: What specific, positive request would you make?
\end{enumerate}
Practice speaking it out loud. Then, in the actual conversation, notice 
how your child responds to being spoken to this way.
\end{practiceexercise}

\begin{reflection}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item What words or phrases do you use habitually that might be 
        evaluations rather than observations? 
  \item What needs of yours tend to go unmet in your parenting role? How 
        do those unmet needs affect how you communicate?
  \item In your last significant conflict with your child, what was their 
        underlying need? Did you respond to it?
\end{enumerate}
\end{reflection}
