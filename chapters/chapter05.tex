\chapter{Protective Use of Force}

\begin{quote}
\itshape ``The intention behind the protective use of force is only to protect, not to punish, blame, or condemn.''\\
\upshape --- Marshall B. Rosenberg
\end{quote}

\newpage
\section{Definition}

In the journey of empathetic parenting, we strive to resolve conflicts and meet needs through empathetic communication and mutual understanding. 
However, there are moments when words are insufficient or time is too short to engage in dialogue. 
In these acute situations, parents may need to employ what Nonviolent Communication (NVC) terms the ``protective use of force'' \citep{rosenberg2003}. 
It is crucial to distinguish this concept clearly from the punitive use of force. 
The protective use of force is deployed solely to protect life, prevent imminent injury, or secure fundamental rights. 
It is never used to punish, teach a lesson, or assert dominance over a child.

When we use punitive force, the underlying assumption is that the child is ``bad'' or ``wrong'' and deserves to suffer to learn a lesson. 
This approach relies on fear and coercion, which neuroscience shows activates the child's amygdala and triggers a fight, flight, or freeze response \citep{siegel2011}. 
Punitive force undermines trust and damages the parent-child connection. 
In contrast, the protective use of force is rooted in an assumption of ignorance or lack of capacity, not malicious intent \citep{rosenberg2003}. 
We recognize that the child, in that moment, lacks the physical coordination, impulse control, or awareness of danger necessary to act safely. 
Our intervention is a physical extension of our care, aiming to keep them safe while their own regulatory systems catch up.

\begin{keyinsight}
The protective use of force is a physical intervention used exclusively to prevent imminent harm. 
Unlike punitive force, which seeks to cause suffering or teach a lesson through fear, protective force aims only to safeguard the child's well-being and is grounded in empathy for their current lack of capacity or awareness.
\end{keyinsight}

\section{When to Use Protective Force?}

The application of protective force is strictly reserved for situations where immediate physical danger is present, and verbal communication is either impossible or simply too slow to prevent harm \citep{rosenberg2003}.
Consider the classic example of a toddler suddenly darting toward a busy street.
In that critical split second, deep subcortical drives can overwhelm a young child's actions before any logical thought occurs \citep{panksepp1998}.
A parent does not have the luxury to stop and discuss the dangers of traffic or to empathetically explore the child's desire to chase a bouncing ball.
Instead, the parent must physically restrain the child, perhaps by quickly grabbing their arm or scooping them up, to prevent a tragedy.
The force used is exactly what is necessary to ensure safety, and nothing more.

Other common scenarios might include a child about to touch a hot stove, an older sibling aggressively hitting a baby, or a child attempting to jump from a dangerous height.
In each of these acute cases, the child's immediate emotional impulses or environmental triggers create a direct threat to themselves or others \citep{lobue2022}.
Children in these moments often experience a complete lack of reasoning, rendering them unable to foresee the dangerous consequences of their actions \citep{ruba2020}.
Their prefrontal cortex, the area of the brain responsible for logical reasoning and predicting outcomes, is either not fully developed or is temporarily hijacked by intense emotional arousal \citep{gottman1997}.

Furthermore, the immature neurobiological development of impulse control in early childhood means that children cannot always reliably halt their own unsafe behaviors \citep{shonkoff2000}.
This nascent state of self-regulation often necessitates direct external support from a caregiver to maintain physical safety \citep{howard2024}.
As a result, the child effectively loses the integration between their emotional impulses and their logical brain, requiring immediate physical intervention \citep{siegel2011}.
In situations where empathy and foresight are developmentally or situationally absent, direct adult intervention is required to guide and enforce safe boundaries \citep{decety2021}.
Protective force thus acts temporarily as an external prefrontal cortex, providing the necessary physical boundary that the child currently cannot provide for themselves.
It remains a measure of last resort, utilized only when the luxury of time and empathetic dialogue is entirely absent.

\begin{keyinsight}
Protective force is employed only in \textbf{emergencies} to prevent immediate physical harm, acting as an external boundary when a child's own impulse control or understanding of danger is insufficient.
It is used when time or circumstances do not allow for verbal communication or empathetic dialogue.
\end{keyinsight}

\section{The Mindset Behind Protective Force}

The defining characteristic of protective force lies not just in the action itself, but in the mindset of the parent employing it.
To use force protectively, a parent must remain connected to their empathetic core, even while taking swift, physical action.
This requires significant emotional regulation \citep{siegel2011}.
If a parent grabs a child in anger, fueled by thoughts like ``How could you be so stupid!'' or ``You never listen!'', the intervention easily slips into punishment, even if the initial goal was safety.
The child will feel the anger and judgment in the parent's touch, which can be traumatizing.

To maintain a protective mindset, parents must focus intensely on the objective need for safety rather than evaluating the child's behavior \citep{rosenberg2003}.
The internal narrative should be, ``I need to keep you safe right now,'' rather than, ``You are being bad.''
This subtle but profound shift in intention alters the energy behind the physical intervention.
A parent using protective force might firmly hold a hitting child while internally or externally communicating, ``I won't let you hit. Hitting hurts. I am holding your hands to keep everyone safe.''
The firmness is combined with a protective, non-judgmental presence, preventing the escalation of fear and shame.

\begin{keyinsight}
The efficacy and safety of protective force depend entirely on the parent's mindset.
It requires the parent to manage their own fear and anger, focusing purely on the objective need for safety without judging or evaluating the child.
The physical intervention must be accompanied by an internal stance of care and protection.
\end{keyinsight}

\section{Reconnecting After Using Force}

Because the protective use of force involves a physical overriding of a child's autonomy, it often causes a rupture in the connection, even when done with the best intentions.
The child may feel scared, angry, or frustrated by the physical restraint.
Therefore, the critical final step in the protective use of force is repairing the relationship and reconnecting once the immediate danger has passed \citep{siegel2003, gottman1997}.
Ignoring the child's emotional response leaves them to process the intense physical intervention alone, which can lead to confusion and mistrust.

Reconnection begins with acknowledging and validating the child's feelings.
A parent might say, ``You were so scared when I grabbed you,'' or ``You are really angry that I picked you up when you wanted to run.''
Once the child feels heard, the parent can calmly explain the reason for the protective action, emphasizing the need for safety.
``I grabbed your arm because a car was coming, and I needed to keep you safe. My job is to protect you.''
This process helps the child make sense of the event, integrating their emotional experience with a logical understanding of the danger \citep{siegel2011}.
It transforms a potentially frightening physical intervention into a reaffirmation of the parent's protective love and commitment.

\begin{keyinsight}
Using physical force, even protectively, often causes emotional distress and a rupture in connection.
It is essential to engage in repair once safety is secured by validating the child's feelings of fear or anger and calmly explaining that the action was taken solely out of a need to protect them.
\end{keyinsight}

\section{Practice Exercise}
\begin{practiceexercise}
\textbf{Multiple Choice Questions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item What is the primary intention behind the protective use of force?
    \begin{itemize}
      \item[A)] To teach the child a valuable lesson about consequences.
      \item[B)] To establish parental dominance and authority.
      \item[C)] To solely protect life, prevent injury, or secure rights.
      \item[D)] To punish the child for unsafe behavior.
    \end{itemize}
  \item Which scenario best justifies the use of protective force?
    \begin{itemize}
      \item[A)] A child refusing to eat their vegetables at dinner.
      \item[B)] A toddler suddenly running toward a busy street.
      \item[C)] A teenager ignoring a request to clean their room.
      \item[D)] A child talking back disrespectfully.
    \end{itemize}
  \item How does the protective use of force differ from punitive force regarding the child's intent?
    \begin{itemize}
      \item[A)] Protective force assumes the child is acting with malicious intent.
      \item[B)] Protective force assumes the child lacks capacity or awareness, not malicious intent.
      \item[C)] Punitive force assumes the child is just clumsy.
      \item[D)] There is no difference; both assume the child is misbehaving on purpose.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
\vspace{\baselineskip}

\textbf{Explanations for Questions 1--3}

\textbf{Question 1: C.} The protective use of force aims exclusively to prevent harm or protect life. Options A, B, and D describe the goals of punitive force, which seeks to punish or control through fear.

\textbf{Question 2: B.} A toddler running into a street represents an immediate, severe physical danger where communication is too slow, necessitating protective force. Options A, C, and D involve behavioral issues that do not pose an imminent physical threat and should be addressed through dialogue.

\textbf{Question 3: B.} Protective force is rooted in understanding that the child currently lacks the ability or awareness to stay safe. Punitive force often incorrectly assumes the child is deliberately being ``bad'' and needs to be punished.
